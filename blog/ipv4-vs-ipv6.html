IPv4 vs IPv6: What's the Difference?

NetPeek · Updated August 28, 2026

IP addresses come in two versions: IPv4 and IPv6. Most people never think about it, but the version you use affects how your device connects to the internet. Here's everything you need to know, explained simply.

Not sure which one you're using? Check with our free IP address tool — it shows your public IP address and whether it's IPv4 or IPv6.

The short answer

IPv4 is the original addressing system, using 32-bit numbers written like 192.168.1.1. IPv6 is the modern successor, using 128-bit addresses written in hexadecimal like 2001:db8:3333:4444:5555:6666:7777:8888. IPv6 was created mainly because we simply ran out of IPv4 addresses.

Key differences between IPv4 and IPv6

IPv4IPv6
Address size32 bits128 bits
FormatDecimal, dotted (e.g. 192.168.1.1)Hexadecimal, colon-separated (e.g. 2001:db8::1)
Number of addresses4.3 billion340 undecillion (practically unlimited)
SecurityRelies on external layersBuilt-in IPsec support
ConfigurationOften manual or via DHCPAutomatic (stateless autoconfiguration)

Why does IPv6 exist?

The internet was designed when fewer people were online. IPv4 can only provide about 4.3 billion unique addresses — and with billions of smartphones, laptops and smart devices connected, that limit was hit years ago. IPv6's 128-bit format provides a virtually unlimited pool of 340 undecillion addresses, so every device can have its own unique address without workarounds like network address translation (NAT).

How to tell if you're using IPv4 or IPv6

It's easy to tell just by looking at the format of an address:

  • IPv4 uses numbers and dots, e.g. 192.168.0.1.
  • IPv6 uses letters and numbers separated by colons, e.g. 2606:4700:4700::1111.

Check your public address with our free IP tool — if the result is short and dotted, you're on IPv4; if it's long and includes letters and colons, you're on IPv6.

Do I need to do anything?

No. Your devices, routers and operating systems handle the switch automatically. Both versions work side by side (a technique called dual stack), and you don't need to configure anything as an end user. IPv6 adoption is steadily growing, but IPv4 will remain in use for many years to come.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is IPv6 faster than IPv4?

Not inherently. IPv6 can sometimes be marginally faster because it removes some routing overhead and NAT, but in practice the difference is negligible for normal browsing. Your connection speed is dominated by your ISP's speed, not the IP version.

Will IPv4 stop working?

Not anytime soon. IPv4 and IPv6 run side by side through dual stack, and IPv4 remains widely supported. The transition is gradual and happens over many years.

Can I find my way around an IPv6 address?

Yes. You can look up an IPv6 address just like an IPv4 one using our DNS lookup tool or an IP lookup service. Many IP tools now support IPv6 addresses.
