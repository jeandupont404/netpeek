DNS Records Explained: A, AAAA, CNAME, MX, NS and TXT Records

NetPeek · Updated August 28, 2026

When you type a domain name into your browser, the Domain Name System (DNS) translates it into the technical records that actually route your request to the right server. These records are the invisible backbone of every website. Here's what the most common DNS record types do.

What is a DNS record?

A DNS record is an instruction stored in a domain's zone file. Together, a domain's records tell the internet where its website lives, where its email goes, and how to verify it. Every time you visit a site, your device queries these records — usually in milliseconds, in the background.

The main DNS record types

A record (Address)

The A record maps a domain name to an IPv4 address. It's the most fundamental record — it's what tells a browser "this domain points to this server's address." For example, an A record might point example.com to 93.184.216.34.

AAAA record

The AAAA record is exactly like an A record, but maps the domain to an IPv6 address instead of IPv4. As the internet adopts IPv6, more sites publish AAAA records alongside their A records.

CNAME record (Canonical Name)

A CNAME record aliases one domain to another, so both resolve to the same place. It's commonly used so that www.example.com points to example.com, and for pointing subdomains to services like a CDN or email provider.

MX record (Mail Exchanger)

The MX record specifies which mail servers are responsible for receiving email for the domain, along with a priority value. Lower numbers mean higher priority. If you run email for a custom domain, correct MX records are essential.

NS record (Name Server)

The NS record identifies the authoritative name servers that hold the domain's DNS zone. It's the record that tells the internet which server is the "source of truth" for a domain's other records.

TXT record (Text)

A TXT record holds arbitrary text attached to a domain. It's most often used for verification and security — for example, SPF and DKIM records that help prevent email spoofing, or tokens that prove you own a domain (like a Google Search Console verification).

Why DNS records matter for your website

If your records are wrong, your site won't load, your email won't arrive, or visitors will be sent to the wrong server. Understanding these records helps you debug "why is my website down?" questions and correctly configure hosting, email and custom domain setups for services like GitHub Pages.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do DNS changes take?

It depends on the TTL (Time To Live) value of the record and your ISP's caching. Changes can take anywhere from minutes to 48 hours to propagate everywhere, though most take effect within a few hours.

What is a DNS TTL?

TTL (Time To Live) is how long a DNS record can be cached before it's re-queried. A shorter TTL means changes propagate faster, while a longer TTL reduces DNS queries and speeds up repeated lookups.

What does DNS propagation mean?

DNS propagation is the time it takes for a record change to spread from your domain's nameservers to all the DNS caches around the world. During propagation, some users may see the old record and others the new one.
